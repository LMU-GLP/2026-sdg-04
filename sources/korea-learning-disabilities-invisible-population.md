# The Invisible Population of Students With Learning Disabilities in South Korea

- **Source:** Mikyung Shin (West Texas A&M University) & Nari Choi (Gwangju National University of Education), *Intervention in School and Clinic* (in press academic paper, provided in repo)
- **URL:** No public URL on file — PDF supplied directly to this repo ("Shin & Choi (in press). The invisible population of students with learning disabilities in South Korea.pdf")
- **Read on:** 2026-07-31

## Key figures

- Citing South Korea's Ministry of Education (MOE) special education statistics: the prevalence rate of students with learning disabilities (LD) among those receiving special education support was **8.7% in 2009**, falling to **only 1.5% in 2019.**
- Students identified with LD represent **only 0.02% of the total school-age population** (MOE, 2019).
- Separately, nationwide surveys report that **"about 1–5% of school-age students struggle with reading and are at risk of having LD"** (Y. Kim, O. Kang, Woo, & Byun, 2015) — i.e., the at-risk population is far larger than the 0.02% who are formally identified.
- A parent-child heritability figure cited elsewhere in the literature: a child of a parent with LD has a **40–60%** chance of also having LD.
- In a public-attitudes survey and social media analysis (K. Lee, 2016), **"more than 60% of respondents said that there was still pervasive discrimination toward people with disabilities."**

## Direct quotes

> "In 2019, only 1.5% of students receiving special education support were identified as having learning disabilities."

> "It is highly likely that the population either has misconceptions of LD or thinks that dyslexia is unrelated to, or not a subcategory of, LD."

> "Despite the invisible prevalence of LD over time, teachers in schools have expressed that they continue to encounter at-risk and LD students in their classes."

## Summary

South Korea's official count of students with learning disabilities has collapsed from 8.7% to 1.5% of special-education recipients over a decade, even as researchers and teachers say the true at-risk population (1–5% of all students) hasn't shrunk — the authors argue this is a measurement and identification failure, not a real decline. The paper frames this as a barrier of a different kind: students who need support are going unrecognized because of unclear legal definitions, inconsistent diagnostic criteria between two competing national standards, and low public understanding of what LD and dyslexia actually are.
