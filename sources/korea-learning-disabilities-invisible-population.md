# The Invisible Population of Students With Learning Disabilities in South Korea

- **Source:** Mikyung Shin & Nari Choi, *Intervention in School and Clinic*, January 2022 (West Texas A&M University / Gwangju National University of Education)
- **URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/10534512211014883
- **Read on:** 2026-07-31 (read via PDF copy provided by the team)

## Key figures

- The prevalence rate of learning disabilities (LD) among South Korean students receiving special education support **fell from 8.7% in 2009 to only 1.5% in 2019** (Ministry of Education, 2019), even as awareness of disability rights increased.
- Students identified with LD represent **only 0.02% of the total school-age population** in South Korea (MOE, 2019).
- Separately, nationwide surveys estimate that **"about 1–5% of school-age students struggle with reading and are at risk of having LD"** (Y. Kim, O. Kang, Woo, & Byun, 2015) — far above the 0.02% actually identified.
- In a 2016 survey and social media analysis, **"more than 60% of respondents said that there was still pervasive discrimination toward people with disabilities"** in South Korea (K. Lee, 2016).
- Globally, "over a billion people (approximately 15% of the world's population) have some degree of disability" (World Health Organization & World Bank, 2011), cited by the authors for context.

## Direct quotes

> "Over the last decade, there has been a rapidly declining trend in the prevalence of students with learning disabilities in South Korea. In 2019, only 1.5% of students receiving special education support were identified as having learning disabilities."

> "In O. Lee and Shin's (2019) study, most high school respondents said they knew about or had heard the term dyslexia, but did not know about LD or other subcategories of LD... It is highly likely that the population either has misconceptions of LD or thinks that dyslexia is unrelated to, or not a subcategory of, LD."

## Why the gap matters

The authors argue the falling identification rate does not mean fewer students struggle — teachers report "they continue to encounter at-risk and LD students in their classes" (W. Kim & J. Kim, 2016) even as official numbers drop. They attribute the gap to three unresolved issues: no consistent legal definition of LD since it entered Korean law in 1994, two competing identification criteria used inconsistently by regional assessment centers since 2010, and low public/teacher understanding of what LD (including dyslexia) actually is.

## Summary

South Korea's official learning-disability identification rate collapsed from 8.7% to 1.5% of special-education students between 2009 and 2019 (just 0.02% of all students), even though separate surveys suggest 1–5% of students actually struggle with reading — meaning most Korean students with a reading-related learning disability are likely never identified or supported. The barrier here is not a lack of a diagnosis system, but unclear definitions, inconsistent identification criteria, and low public awareness that leave affected students invisible.
