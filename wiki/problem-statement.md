# Problem Statement: The "Invisible Population" of Dyslexic Students in Korea's Elementary and Middle Schools

## Who is affected

This project focuses on elementary and middle school students in South Korea who show
signs of dyslexia risk but are never formally screened or diagnosed — the group Shin and
Choi (2019) call the **"invisible population"** of learning disabilities (LD) in Korea
([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md)).
This is a deliberately narrower target than either (a) students already formally diagnosed
with LD, or (b) the broader population of students with general reading/fluency
difficulties who are not necessarily dyslexic — and the distinction matters, because our
own sources report very different numbers for each group.

## How many students

- The Incheon Metropolitan Office of Education / Gyeongin National University of Education
  dyslexia guidebook states: **"우리나라에서도 1~5% 정도의 학생이 난독증을 갖고 있을 것으로
  추정되고 있습니다"** — an estimated 1–5% of Korean students have dyslexia, which the
  guidebook explicitly notes is *lower* than the global estimate of 5–20%.
  ([Incheon dyslexia guidebook](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)) A separate
  nationwide survey cited in the Shin & Choi paper (Y. Kim, Kang, Woo, & Byun, 2015)
  independently reports the same range: **"about 1–5% of school-age students struggle with
  reading and are at risk of having LD."**
  ([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))
- Applying that 1–5% range to Korea's official 2024 enrollment figures — **2,495,005
  elementary students and 1,332,850 middle school students** (combined: 3,827,855)
  ([2024 KEDI enrollment statistics](../sources/kedi-2024-enrollment-statistics.md)) — yields
  an estimated **38,000–191,000** elementary and middle school students nationwide who may
  be at risk of dyslexia. **This headcount is our own arithmetic** (1–5% × 3,827,855), not a
  figure stated directly in any single source — it should be presented as an estimate, not
  a measured count.
- By contrast, only **1.5%** of students receiving special education support were formally
  identified with LD in 2019 (down from **8.7%** in 2009), and identified LD students
  represent just **0.02%** of the total school-age population.
  ([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))
  That gap — an estimated 1–5% at risk vs. only 0.02% formally identified — is the core
  evidence for calling this an "invisible population," and is more defensible than any
  absolute headcount, since it doesn't depend on our own multiplication.

## What our sources say is going wrong (the pipeline problem)

1. **No consistent national early-screening system.** Shin & Choi report that city/provincial
   ordinances supporting dyslexic students (including Incheon's own, in effect since
   2024.7.15) were designed mainly to prevent school dropout, not to create a schoolwide,
   systematic screening pipeline — leaving general education teachers without clear
   guidance on when to refer a struggling reader for full evaluation.
   ([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))
   The Incheon guidebook independently emphasizes that early diagnosis is the single most
   powerful intervention available — **"읽기의 어려움을 보이는 학생에 대한 정확하고 빠른 진단은
   가장 강력하고도 효과적인 개입입니다"** — and cites a 2015 Connecticut longitudinal study
   showing the reading gap between strong and weak readers appears by grade 1 and persists
   through adolescence rather than closing on its own, warning that many students aren't
   identified until 3rd grade or later, by which point the gap has already widened.
   ([Incheon dyslexia guidebook](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md))
2. **Identification is actively declining, not improving.** South Korea's own Ministry of
   Education data show the identified LD rate falling from 8.7% (2009) to 1.5% (2019) of
   special-education students — the opposite direction you'd expect if awareness and
   screening were improving. Shin and Choi attribute this partly to two competing,
   inconsistently applied identification criteria (MOEST 2010 vs. KLDA 2013) used
   selectively across regional support centers, and partly to stigma: a 2016 survey found
   more than 60% of respondents still perceived pervasive discrimination toward people with
   disabilities in Korea, and parents often hide a child's disability as a result.
   ([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))
3. **Even after identification, instructional support requires real intensity.** The Incheon
   guidebook lays out five evidence-based intervention components (phonological awareness,
   phonics, fluency, vocabulary, comprehension) and states effective support requires
   trained teachers delivering systematic, explicit instruction **at least 3 times a week**
   — a level of specialized, sustained support that is difficult to guarantee without a
   reliable identification step feeding into it.
   ([Incheon dyslexia guidebook](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md))

## Context, not core evidence

A 2024 Chungnam Provincial Office of Education study (eye-tracking on 145 middle schoolers
across grades 1–3 and 97 3rd-grade elementary students, per news reporting) found that
**"초등학생의 98%, 중학생의 92%가 정해진 시간 안에 교과서 지문을 읽지 못했다"** — 98% of
elementary students and 92% of middle schoolers tested failed to finish reading a
grade-level textbook passage within the allotted time.
([Chungnam literacy/eye-tracking study](../sources/chungnam-literacy-eyetracking-study.md))
**This is a general reading-fluency/literacy statistic, not a dyslexia-prevalence
statistic**, and it comes from a small regional sample rather than a national study — we
cite it only to show that reading difficulty is a visible, urgent phenomenon in Korean
classrooms, not as evidence about how many students specifically have dyslexia.

## Statement

In South Korea, an estimated 1–5% of the roughly 3.83 million elementary and middle school
students — as many as 38,000 to 191,000 children nationwide, by our own extrapolation — are
likely at risk of dyslexia, yet officially identified learning-disability rates have fallen
to just 0.02% of all students and 1.5% of special-education recipients, a decline driven by
the absence of a consistent national early-screening system, inconsistently applied
identification criteria across regions, and social stigma that discourages families and
teachers from pursuing diagnosis. As a result, most at-risk students remain an "invisible
population" who never receive the evidence-based reading instruction, classroom
accommodations, or adapted materials shown to close their reading gap with peers — and the
longer identification is delayed, the harder that gap becomes to close.
