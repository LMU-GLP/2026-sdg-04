# South Korea: The Cost of Private Education as a Barrier

## The core problem

Korean households spent just under **29.2 trillion won ($20.2 billion) on private education in 2024**, up **60.1% from 2014** — the fourth consecutive annual increase, per Korean Statistical Information Service (KOSIS) / Ministry of Data and Statistics figures reported by Yonhap ([source](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).

By education level, 2014→2024 spending growth:
- Elementary: 7.6 trillion → **13.2 trillion won (+74.1%)** — the biggest jump
- Middle school: → 7.8 trillion won (+40.7%)
- High school: → 8.1 trillion won (+60.5%)

A family with at least two unmarried children spent an average of **611,000 won per month** on private education in Q3 2025 — **12.6% of the family's entire monthly expenditure, second only to food**. That share has climbed steadily: 11.5% (2019) → 12.8% (2024) → 13.0% (Q1 2025) → **13.5% in Q2 2025, a record high** ([source](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).

## Why this counts as a barrier, not just a preference

The source's own explanation is not that families are simply choosing enrichment: "frequent changes to college admission policies and resultant uncertainty are forcing parents to lean more heavily on private education," and separately, hagwons increasingly function as childcare for dual-income households, who "often send their children to after-school hagwons during their working hours rather than leaving them at home alone" ([source](../sources/korea-private-education.md)). That means the cost barrier compounds for families who can't afford it — for both academic and childcare reasons.

## Claims we found but could not verify

The source itself flags several widely circulated figures as unverified: that "78% of students attend hagwons," that Seoul students spend twice what rural students do, or that tutoring costs "10% of household income" and is "the highest percentage worldwide." These appear only in commercial blogs, not government data, and should **not** be used in a pitch ([source](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).

## Related barrier: under-identification of learning disabilities

A separate and distinct barrier in Korea — not a cost barrier — is that officially identified learning-disability rates have fallen even as reading-difficulty surveys suggest a stable at-risk population. See [Dyslexia and Learning Disabilities in Korea](dyslexia-learning-disabilities.md).
