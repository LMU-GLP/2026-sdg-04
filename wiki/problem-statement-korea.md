# Problem Statement (Candidate Region): South Korea

This is a second, fully sourced problem statement — for **South Korea**, alongside the
team's [LAUSD problem statement](problem-statement.md) — so the team can compare both
before committing to a final region, per the challenge brief. It should be read together
with [LA vs. South Korea](la-vs-korea-comparison.md).

## Region

**South Korea**, elementary and middle school students nationwide (not one city or province).

## Who is affected

Students who show signs of dyslexia risk but are never formally screened or diagnosed — the
group Shin and Choi (in press) call the **"invisible population"** of learning disabilities (LD)
in Korea ([source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md)). This is a
deliberately narrower target than either (a) students already formally diagnosed with LD, or
(b) the broader population of students with general reading/fluency difficulties who are not
necessarily dyslexic — our sources report very different numbers for each group, and they
should not be added together or treated as the same population.

## How many students

- The Incheon guidebook estimates roughly **1–5% of Korean school-age students have
  dyslexia**, noting this is lower than a **global estimate of 5–20%**
  ([source](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)). A separate nationwide survey (Y. Kim,
  Kang, Woo, & Byun, 2015, cited in Shin & Choi) reports that about **1–5% of school-age
  students struggle with reading** and are at risk of LD
  ([source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md)).
- Applying that 1–5% range to Korea's official 2024 enrollment figures — **2,495,005**
  elementary students and **1,332,850** middle school students, **3,827,855** combined
  ([source](../sources/korea-2024-enrollment-statistics.md)) — yields an estimated
  **38,000–191,000** elementary and middle school students nationwide who may be at risk of
  dyslexia. **This headcount is our own extrapolation** (1–5% × 3,827,855), not a figure
  reported directly by any single source, and should be presented as an estimate, not a
  measured count.
- By contrast, only **1.5%** of students receiving special-education support were formally
  identified with LD in 2019 (down from **8.7%** in 2009), and identified LD students represent
  just **0.02%** of the total school-age population
  ([source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md)). That gap — an estimated
  1–5% at risk vs. only 0.02% formally identified — is the evidence for calling this an
  "invisible population," and is more defensible than any absolute headcount.

## What our sources say is going wrong (the pipeline problem)

1. **No consistent national early-screening system.** Shin and Choi report that municipal and
   provincial education offices nationwide passed ordinances to support students with dyslexia
   and reading difficulties, but these were "intended to be implemented only as a measure
   designed to prevent student dropouts, rather than as a district-based, schoolwide support
   system" — Gyeonggi-do's 2014 ordinance is their specific example
   ([source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md)). Separately, the Incheon
   guidebook stresses that early diagnosis — ideally at kindergarten or grade 1 — is "the most
   powerful and effective intervention," but that if dyslexia isn't identified until elementary
   3rd grade or later, the reading gap with peers has already grown too wide
   ([source](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)). *(Note: our sources don't establish
   that Incheon's own ordinance was a dropout-prevention-only measure like Gyeonggi-do's — the
   Incheon guidebook instead documents legally grounded classroom accommodations. Don't
   conflate the two offices' ordinances.)*
2. **Identification is declining, not improving.** South Korea's own Ministry of Education data
   show the identified LD rate falling from 8.7% (2009) to 1.5% (2019) of special-education
   students — the opposite of what you'd expect if screening were improving. Shin and Choi
   attribute this partly to two competing, inconsistently applied identification criteria — MOEST
   (2010) and KLDA (2013) — used selectively across regional support centers (SESCs), and
   partly to stigma: more than 60% of respondents in one 2016 survey said discrimination toward
   people with disabilities was still pervasive
   ([source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md)).
3. **Support after identification is inconsistent.** The Incheon guidebook lays out
   evidence-based intervention components (phonological awareness, phonics, fluency,
   vocabulary, comprehension) and stresses effective support requires trained teachers
   delivering systematic, explicit instruction at least three times a week
   ([source](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)) — a level of sustained, specialized
   support that's hard to guarantee without a reliable identification step feeding into it.

## Context, not core evidence

A 2024 Chungnam Provincial Office of Education study (with 난독과 문해력 연구소), using
eye-tracking on 145 middle schoolers and 97 elementary 3rd-graders, found that **92% of middle
school students and 98% of elementary students** failed to finish reading a grade-level
textbook passage within the allotted time
([source](../sources/chungnam-reading-literacy-study-2024.md)). This is a general
reading-fluency/literacy statistic from a small regional sample, **not a dyslexia-prevalence
statistic** and not a national study — cited only to show reading difficulty is a visible,
urgent phenomenon in Korean classrooms, not as evidence of how many students specifically have
dyslexia.

## The problem statement

In South Korea, an estimated 1–5% of the roughly 3.83 million elementary and middle school
students — as many as 38,000 to 191,000 children nationwide — are likely at risk of dyslexia,
yet officially identified learning-disability rates have fallen to just 0.02% of all students
and 1.5% of special-education recipients. Our sources point to three drivers of that gap: the
absence of a consistent national early-screening system (existing ordinances were built for
dropout prevention, not schoolwide screening), inconsistently applied identification criteria
across regions (MOEST 2010 vs. KLDA 2013), and social stigma that discourages families and
teachers from pursuing diagnosis. As a result, most at-risk students remain an "invisible
population" who never receive the evidence-based reading instruction, classroom
accommodations, or adapted materials shown to close their reading gap with peers — and the
longer identification is delayed, the harder that gap becomes to close.

## What we still can't defend, and shouldn't claim in a pitch

- The 38,000–191,000 headcount is our own multiplication of a survey-based percentage range by
  a national enrollment total — it is not a number any single source reports, and shouldn't be
  quoted as if it were a measured count.
- Which of the two "1–5%" figures (dyslexia specifically, vs. general reading difficulty/LD
  risk) a given at-risk student falls into — our sources use both phrasings for what may or may
  not be the same population.
- Whether Incheon's own ordinance shares Gyeonggi-do's dropout-prevention-only framing — Shin &
  Choi's critique names Gyeonggi-do specifically, and the Incheon guidebook describes legally
  grounded classroom accommodations instead, so we should not present them as the same thing.
- Any causal link between the Chungnam eye-tracking study's reading-fluency findings and
  dyslexia specifically — no source draws that connection; it is a distinct, general-literacy
  finding from a small regional sample.
- Comparing this Korea headcount to the LAUSD ~128,000-student estimate directly — the two
  figures are measured completely differently (a district resolution's "signs or symptoms"
  estimate vs. our own extrapolation from national survey percentages), so they should not be
  read side by side as comparable. See [LA vs. South Korea](la-vs-korea-comparison.md).
