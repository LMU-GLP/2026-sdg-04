# Los Angeles vs. South Korea: Comparing the Evidence, Not the Numbers

The team hasn't picked a region yet. Here's what each region's evidence base actually
supports — and a warning about what it doesn't let us say.

## Los Angeles: the barrier is getting into the classroom

- Chronic absenteeism (missing >10% of the school year) hit **"just over 45%"** of
  LAUSD students in 2021–22, falling to **"more than 32%"** by 2023–24 — still above
  the national peak of 28% (2022)
  ([LA chronic absenteeism](la-chronic-absenteeism.md),
  [source](../sources/la-chronic-absenteeism.md)).
- The intervention that's working, per the district, is unglamorous and specific:
  home visits, free busing, uniforms — not a new curriculum or app
  ([source](../sources/la-chronic-absenteeism.md)).
- California separately has a *legal* gap around reading-difficulty accommodation:
  screening is now mandated (2025–2026), but naming what accommodation to actually
  provide is left to individual schools
  ([LucidRead](dyslexia-support-tools.md), [source](../sources/lucidread-dyslexia-extension.md)).
  Only **29% of California 4th graders** read at or above proficient
  ([source](../sources/lucidread-dyslexia-extension.md)).

## South Korea: the barrier is cost, pressure, and invisibility

- Families now spend a **record 13.5%** of their monthly household budget (Q2 2025) on
  private tutoring, second only to food, and total national private-education spending
  rose 60.1% in a decade
  ([Korea private education](korea-private-education-pressure.md),
  [source](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).
- Separately, and more specifically to *learning barriers*: only **1.5%** of
  special-education students (0.02% of all students) are identified with a learning
  disability, despite estimates that **1–5%** of Korean students struggle with reading
  at a dyslexia-consistent level — a gap the evidence attributes to unstable legal
  definitions, inconsistent identification criteria, and public misunderstanding, not
  to fewer students actually struggling
  ([Dyslexia and LD in Korea](dyslexia-learning-disabilities.md),
  [source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md)).
- A 2025 government guidebook exists specifically because teachers and parents lacked
  guidance on diagnosis and instruction
  ([source](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)).

## What we can't say

We are **not** comparing LA's 32% absenteeism rate to Korea's 13.5% household spending
share, or to Korea's 1.5% LD identification rate — these are three different
measurements of three different things (an attendance rate, a share of household
budget, and a diagnostic identification rate), and CLAUDE.md's rule against comparing
unlike figures across countries applies here directly. We also don't have a Korean
chronic-absenteeism figure or an LA learning-disability identification figure to set
against these, so a true side-by-side on either dimension isn't possible with the
sources currently in this wiki.

## What the comparison does tell us

Both regions have real, well-documented barriers — and in both, the strongest
documented intervention is not a new piece of technology but *systemic follow-through*:
LA's home-visit program, and Korea's push for consistent diagnostic criteria and
teacher training. If the team's SDG 4 challenge centers on **dyslexia/reading
disabilities specifically**, South Korea currently has the deeper evidence trail (an
academic study plus a government guidebook); if it centers on **school engagement and
access more broadly**, Los Angeles has the deeper trail (a specific, quantified,
multi-year district program). A third option is to scope the project around dyslexia
support *tools*, where both regions already have live examples — LucidRead in
California, the Incheon guidebook in Korea — worth comparing directly.
