# South Korea: The Cost and Pressure of Private Education

In South Korea, the barrier to accessible learning looks different from Los Angeles —
it's not about missing school, but about a private tutoring system ("hagwon" culture)
that consumes a rising share of family budgets and time
([Korea private education spending](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).

## The scale of the spending

Korean households spent **"just under 29.2 trillion won ($20.2 billion) on private
education in 2024, up 60.1% from 2014"** — the fourth consecutive annual increase
([Korea private education spending](../sources/korea-private-education.md)). Elementary
spending grew fastest of all: from 7.6 trillion won in 2014 to **13.2 trillion won
(+74.1%)** in 2024, versus +40.7% for middle school and +60.5% for high school
([Korea private education spending](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).

Private education is now a dominant line item in household budgets: a family with at
least two unmarried children spent an average of **611,000 won per month** in Q3 2025,
or **"12.6% of the family's entire monthly expenditure — second only to food."** The
share of monthly household spending going to private education has climbed every year
tracked: 11.5% (2019) → 12.8% (2024) → 13.0% (Q1 2025) → **13.5% in Q2 2025, a record
high** ([Korea private education spending](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).

## Why spending keeps rising

The source cites two government-given reasons: families have more disposable income to
spend on extra lessons, and rising numbers of dual-income households "often send their
children to after-school hagwons during their working hours rather than leaving them
at home alone" — meaning hagwons are partly functioning as childcare, not just academic
supplementation ([Korea private education spending](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).

## A caution on numbers we didn't use

The source itself flags that widely circulated claims — "78% of students attend
hagwons," Seoul students spending twice what rural students do, or tutoring costing
"10% of household income... the highest percentage worldwide" — appear only on
commercial blogs, not in the government release, and could not be verified
([Korea private education spending](../sources/korea-private-education.md)). We're
leaving them out per this wiki's citation rule.

## How this connects to a different, less visible barrier

Private education spending measures a *financial and time* barrier for the general
student population. It is a separate issue from South Korea's specific, and largely
unaddressed, barrier for students with dyslexia or other learning disabilities — see
[Dyslexia and Learning Disabilities in South Korea](dyslexia-learning-disabilities.md).
