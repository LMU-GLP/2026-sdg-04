# Dyslexia and Learning Disabilities as a Barrier to Learning

## Scale of the problem

Global estimate: **5% to 20% of all students** are estimated to have dyslexia, per the Incheon guidebook ([source](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)). In Korea specifically, that guidebook estimates **1–5% of students** have dyslexia, while a separate 2015 nationwide survey cited by Shin & Choi found **about 1–5% of school-age students struggle with reading** and are at risk of a learning disability ([source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md)).

## The identification gap

Despite that estimated at-risk population, South Korea's *officially identified* learning-disability rate has been falling for over a decade: **8.7%** of students receiving special-education support were identified with a learning disability in 2009, versus just **1.5%** in 2019 — representing only **0.02% of the entire school-age population** ([source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md)).

Shin & Choi argue this falling rate is not a real decline in need — it reflects an unresolved legal and clinical debate: two different identification criteria have been used inconsistently by Korean special education support centers since 2010, and public understanding of learning disabilities remains limited (more than 60% of respondents in one 2016 survey said discrimination toward people with disabilities was still pervasive) ([source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md)).

## Why this matters if untreated

The Incheon guidebook cites Shaywitz's 2015 Connecticut longitudinal study: once a reading-score gap opens between strong and weak readers, it **persists essentially unchanged from about age 6 through age 16** without intervention — described as a chronic difficulty, not a temporary delay ([source](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)).

## Genetics and family risk

If either parent has dyslexia, a child's probability of having it is about **40–60%**; if one sibling has dyslexia, the others' probability is **3–10 times higher** than otherwise ([source](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)).

## What effective intervention looks like

The Incheon guidebook recommends structured, phonics-based instruction (rather than "whole-word" methods) delivered **at least 3 times per week**, 10–15 minutes per session for phonological awareness work, alongside standardized screening (a 5-minute web-based test, teacher checklists) and legally-grounded classroom accommodations: extra test time, text-to-speech/audiobooks, oral exam options, reduced homework, and permitted keyboard use ([source](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)).

## A U.S. data point, not directly comparable

Separately, California's 2024 NAEP reading results show only **29% of 4th graders reading at proficient level** ([source](../sources/lucidread-dyslexia-accommodations.md)). This measures general reading proficiency across all students, not dyslexia/LD identification specifically — it should not be read as a Korea-vs-California dyslexia comparison.
