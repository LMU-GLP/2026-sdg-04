# Dyslexia and Learning Disabilities: A Barrier That Goes Unrecognized

Across our sources, the clearest documented barrier to accessible learning — for
either candidate region — is dyslexia and other reading-related learning
disabilities (LD). Two very different kinds of sources converge on the same problem
in South Korea: an academic paper analyzing national identification statistics, and a
2025 government instructional guidebook written because teachers and parents still
lack basic guidance.

## The size of the problem, and the size of the gap

A peer-reviewed study of Korean Ministry of Education data found that the share of
special-education students identified with a learning disability **"fell from 8.7% in
2009 to only 1.5% in 2019"** — even as disability-rights awareness increased over the
same period. That 1.5% works out to just **"0.02% of the total school-age
population"**
([The Invisible Population of Students With LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md)).

That is a strikingly small number next to a separate line of survey research the same
paper cites, which estimates **"about 1–5% of school-age students struggle with
reading and are at risk of having LD"**
([The Invisible Population of Students With LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md)) —
and next to the 2025 Incheon guidebook's own estimate that, worldwide, "an estimated
5% to 20% of all students have dyslexia," and "in our country [South Korea] as well, an
estimated 1–5% of students are believed to have dyslexia"
([Incheon dyslexia guidebook](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)).

Put plainly: the two sources agree that roughly **1 in 20 to 1 in 100 Korean students**
is estimated to struggle with reading at a level consistent with dyslexia or LD, but
only about **2 in 10,000** are formally identified. Neither source explains that gap
away with any single cause — the academic paper attributes it to a legal definition
that has never stabilized (revised twice since 1994), two competing identification
criteria used inconsistently since 2010, and low public and teacher understanding of
what LD even is
([The Invisible Population of Students With LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md)).
That last point is echoed directly: in a national survey, most Korean high schoolers
"knew about or had heard the term *dyslexia*, but did not know about LD or other
subcategories of LD," suggesting many people don't realize dyslexia even is a learning
disability
([The Invisible Population of Students With LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md)).

## Stigma is part of the barrier, not a side effect

In a 2016 survey and social-media analysis, "more than 60% of respondents said that
there was still pervasive discrimination toward people with disabilities" in South
Korea, and parents "will choose to hide their children's disabilities unless
appropriate services are provided"
([The Invisible Population of Students With LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md)).
That creates a feedback loop: low identification feeds low awareness, which feeds
stigma, which discourages families from seeking identification in the first place.

## What the evidence says actually helps

The Incheon guidebook — jointly published by a city education office and a national
teacher-training university specifically because existing teacher/parent guidance was
inadequate — lays out what the research base (the U.S. National Reading Panel's five
pillars: phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, comprehension) says works:
early, systematic, and explicit instruction, "at least 3 sessions per week, minimum,"
plus school accommodations like extra time, text-to-speech/audiobooks, oral exams, and
reduced workload
([Incheon dyslexia guidebook](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)). It also
explicitly debunks popular but unproven "fixes": "visual perception training, eye
exercises, colored lenses, and auditory training have not been proven effective as
dyslexia treatments"
([Incheon dyslexia guidebook](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)) — a useful
guardrail against solutions that sound plausible but aren't evidence-based.

Dyslexia also has a strong heritable component: "if either parent has dyslexia, the
probability their child has dyslexia is 40–60%," and if a sibling has it, other
siblings' risk is "about 3 to 10 times higher"
([Incheon dyslexia guidebook](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)) — meaning
family history is a practical, low-cost screening signal schools could use to flag
at-risk students earlier.
