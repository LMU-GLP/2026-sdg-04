# Learning Disabilities in South Korea: An "Invisible" Population

Private tutoring spending ([Private Education Spending](private-education-spending-korea.md))
assumes a student who can access and benefit from more instruction. For students with an
undiagnosed reading or learning disability, more hagwon hours may not be the barrier — being
correctly identified in the first place is.

## The numbers

South Korea's Ministry of Education (MOE) special-education statistics show the prevalence
rate of students identified with learning disabilities (LD), among those receiving special
education support, **fell from 8.7% in 2009 to only 1.5% in 2019.**
([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))

Students formally identified with LD represent **only 0.02% of the total school-age
population.** ([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))

That is strikingly out of step with a separate, independent estimate: nationwide surveys
report that **"about 1–5% of school-age students struggle with reading and are at risk of
having LD."** ([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))
In other words, the population estimated to be at risk (1–5%) is 50 to 250 times larger than
the population actually being identified and served (0.02%).

A 2016 public-attitudes survey found **"more than 60% of respondents said that there was
still pervasive discrimination toward people with disabilities"** in Korea.
([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))

## Why the researchers say the decline is a measurement failure, not a real one

The paper's authors (Shin & Choi) identify three causes: an unclear, twice-revised legal
definition of LD; two competing, inconsistently-applied national identification criteria
(from the Ministry of Education and from the Korean Learning Disabilities Association); and
low public understanding — most surveyed high schoolers had heard of "dyslexia" but didn't
know it was a subcategory of LD at all.
([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))

> "Despite the invisible prevalence of LD over time, teachers in schools have expressed that
> they continue to encounter at-risk and LD students in their classes."
> ([Invisible Population of Students with LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md))
