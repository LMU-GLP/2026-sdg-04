# Overview: Barriers to Learning — LA and South Korea

Our challenge: **how might we make learning more accessible, engaging, or useful for students who face barriers at school?** This page synthesizes what the sources in this wiki actually show, across the two candidate regions that were compared before the team committed to LAUSD (see [Problem Statement](problem-statement.md)).

## Two regions, two very different kinds of barrier

The five sources in this wiki split into two clusters that don't measure the same thing:

- **Los Angeles**: the documented barrier is **attendance** — students physically not being in school often enough to learn, tracked as chronic absenteeism ([LA source](../sources/la-chronic-absenteeism.md)), plus a broader U.S./California reading-proficiency figure from the [LucidRead source](../sources/lucidread-dyslexia-accommodations.md).
- **South Korea**: the documented barriers are **cost** (private tutoring spending eating record shares of household budgets, [Korea education cost source](../sources/korea-private-education.md)) and **under-identification of learning disabilities/dyslexia** — students who are in their seats but not being recognized or supported ([Shin & Choi](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md), [Incheon guidebook](../sources/incheon-dyslexia-guidebook.md)).

These are not the same barrier measured twice in two places — see [LA vs. South Korea](la-vs-korea-comparison.md) for why they can't be directly compared.

## What surprised us in the sources

- In LA, the barrier isn't a lack of programs — LAUSD already runs an attendance-recovery program (iAttend) and the superintendent personally visits homes. The remaining gap (just over 32% chronically absent as of 2023–24) is what's left *after* that effort ([source](../sources/la-chronic-absenteeism.md)).
- In South Korea, the *official* count of students with learning disabilities has been falling for over a decade (8.7% of special-ed students in 2009 → 1.5% in 2019), even though separate surveys estimate 1–5% of *all* school-age students struggle with reading. The researchers argue this is a measurement failure, not a real decline ([source](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-prevalence.md)).
- Korean households are spending a record 13.5% of their monthly budget on private tutoring (Q2 2025) — second only to food — partly because hagwons now double as childcare for dual-income families, not purely as academic supplements ([source](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).

## What we still don't have

- No source here gives LA-specific data on learning-disability identification, or Korea-specific data on chronic absenteeism — so we can't yet say which region's barrier is "bigger" in any comparable sense.
- The Korea private-education source explicitly flags several widely repeated statistics (e.g., "78% of students attend hagwons") that could not be verified in government data and should not be cited ([source](../sources/korea-private-education.md)).

## Relevant UN targets

SDG Target 4.a calls for "safe, non-violent, inclusive and effective learning environments for all," and Target 4.1 for all children achieving "minimum proficiency in reading and mathematics" — the two targets most directly engaged by the evidence here ([source](../sources/un-sdg4-targets.md)).
