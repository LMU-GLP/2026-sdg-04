# Programs and Tools Already Working on This: LucidRead (California)

Dyslexia isn't only a South Korean policy gap — California has its own version of the
same problem: a legal requirement to find at-risk readers, with no funded plan for
what to do once you've found them. One existing tool built directly into that gap is
**LucidRead**, a free Chrome browser extension
([LucidRead](../sources/lucidread-dyslexia-extension.md)).

## The gap it was built for

California passed **SB 114 (2023)**, which brought "K–2 universal screening for
reading difficulties including dyslexia risk" starting in the **2025–2026 school
year**, building on AB 1369 (2015), which had required the state to develop dyslexia
guidelines in the first place
([LucidRead](../sources/lucidread-dyslexia-extension.md)). Despite that, California
"ranked 40th nationally" to implement universal screening, and only **"29% of
California 4th graders" read at or above Proficient**
([LucidRead](../sources/lucidread-dyslexia-extension.md)). Critically, the law requires
schools to *name specific accommodations* in each student's IEP, but sets **no
state-level mandate for what those accommodations should actually be** — leaving
individual teachers and districts to figure it out
([LucidRead](../sources/lucidread-dyslexia-extension.md)).

## What it does

LucidRead targets three difficulties it identifies as core to dyslexia — decoding,
visual stress (which the site says affects "20% of the general population" during
reading), and tracking loss — with in-browser tools: syllable splitting, text-to-speech,
color overlays, a reading ruler, and morpheme highlighting. It requires no account, no
configuration, and collects no data, which the maker describes as "FERPA and COPPA
compliant by design"
([LucidRead](../sources/lucidread-dyslexia-extension.md)).

## Why this belongs next to the Korean sources

Compare this to South Korea's own answer to the same underlying problem, the
[Incheon dyslexia guidebook](dyslexia-learning-disabilities.md): where California's
gap is *lack of a defined accommodation standard* after screening, Korea's gap is
*inconsistent identification criteria before* screening even happens
([The Invisible Population of Students With LD in South Korea](../sources/korea-learning-disabilities-invisible-population.md)).
Both regions have real, current interventions — a free browser tool in one case, a
state-published instructional guidebook in the other — but neither source describes a
national policy that closes the entire pipeline from screening to accommodation. That
gap is worth probing further before committing to a region or a solution.
